Ask people abroad about Germany and you usually get three answers: the Autobahn, Oktoberfest and bureaucracy. The bureaucracy part is fair (more on that later), but the rest is a small slice of what the country actually is. One thing to clear up first: Oktoberfest is a Bavarian thing, not a German one. Plenty of Germans will tell you dead serious, that Bavaria could be handed over to Austria, since it has more in common with those over there than with Germany.

Since I'm on the internet for large amounts of time, I often see videos of people imitating Germans and doing videos with stereotypes. Many times, I don't even chuckle and just get angry, not because I'm German and have no humor, but because I see the reality and not... whatever _that_ is supposed to be.

So here's what Germany is actually dealing with right now, as I see it from my early twenties.

## Politics is splitting people apart

A lot of people have slipped into black-and-white thinking and it shows in how they treat each other. You have a different opinion? Then I'm not talking to you anymore, or you're dead to me. But someone's vote is one part of a person, not all of them.

It gets worse because many people, too many in my opinion, have made their political views their entire personality. Once your politics is your identity, every disagreement feels like an attack on you and that feeds the exact problem I just described.

## Our car industry fell asleep

Germany used to be the country of cars. Then the industry got comfortable. It leaned on government subsidies, worked under the motto "the state will save us" and put little effort into innovation, unless you count selling subscriptions for heated seats.

The result: almost every other carmaker in the world has left us behind on innovation and on affordable cars. If you want a cheap everyday car, you won't find a new one and you definitely won't find a German one.

## New taxes on sugar, plastic and crypto

The finance ministry is planning several new taxes. The sugar tax caused the biggest fight. The ministry wanted to tax not just soft drinks but also juices, iced teas, oat drinks and instant coffee. After a wave of criticism, Finance Minister Klingbeil said of sugar-free drinks, "That won't happen." A tax on drinks that contain sugar is still on the table, though. At one point the Chancellor's Office said the plans "would not garner a majority" in the Union, the conservative half of the governing coalition. The planned plastic and crypto taxes are getting criticism too. I bet people would reinstantiate Killdozer if that would be announced in the US...

## Half of what you pay for gas goes to the state

If you pay €2 per liter of gasoline (about $8.70 per US gallon), roughly 96 to 98 cents of that goes to the government. That's about 32 cents of value-added tax, about 51 cents of energy tax and somewhere around 13 to 15 cents for the CO2 price.

It isn't even more only because of a temporary energy tax cut of 14.04 cents per liter (about 17 cents once you add VAT). That cut runs out at the end of December. If it isn't extended, prices go up in January and in 2027 the CO2 price will be set by the market for the first time, which most people expect to push it higher. The federal government says it will bring in a fuel price cap by January 1 at the latest, modeled on Luxembourg and Belgium. Critics doubt it can do much against rising wholesale and refinery prices. Experts' opionions have been ignored successfully ever since.

## Political violence is rising and some attacks could have been prevented

Politically motivated crime hit a new record in 2025. The Federal Criminal Police Office (BKA) counted 85,837 offenses. Right-wing offenses dropped slightly, while left-wing offenses and violent crimes went up a lot. According to the domestic intelligence service, violent acts by left-wing extremists rose by \~60 percent to 856 cases.

Yet somehow, at the same time, overall violent crime fell slightly in 2025, by 2.3 percent, the first drop since 2021. But non-German suspects still make up 42.9 percent of violent crime suspects, far more than their share of the population. That number comes up in almost every political debate. (Keep in mind these are suspects, not convictions and age, gender and social situation explain part of the gap)

What really gets to me: in several serious stabbings and violent attacks in recent years, the attackers were already known to the authorities as mentally ill, violent or due for deportation and nobody stopped them. The best-known case is Aschaffenburg, where a mentally ill man from Afghanistan is accused of killing a two-year-old boy and a man (there's evidence, but he's  not officially convicted yet). Only after the crime do people like him end up in a locked psychiatric ward as "not criminally responsible". That placement has no fixed end date and according to the Bavarian social ministry it costs around €400 a day, so almost €150,000 per bed per year, paid by taxpayers. Meanwhile the victims and their families are left to deal with what happened on their own. A state that only acts once people are dead is simply not acceptable to a lot of citizens anymore, me included.

## The welfare system is crippled and wont get better any time soon

Our social system is cracking and politicians are watching it happen. Nearly half of all people on basic income support (Bürgergeld) don't have a German passport. Among people on basic old-age support, the share of foreign nationals rose from about 27 percent at the end of 2020 to over 36 percent in March 2026.

To be fair, immigration also brings people who pay in. About 5.9 million of the roughly 35 million people paying social security contributions have a foreign passport and in some eastern states job growth now comes entirely from foreign workers. But many of them work in low-paid helper jobs, so they put relatively little into the system.

The bigger problem is that the baby boomers are retiring and fewer young people come after them (Can't blame people for leaving the country and not wanting to subject their kids with all of the things on this short list). Economists warn that without reforms, total social security contributions could climb above 50 percent of wages within ten years. Guess who pays for that: the people who get up and go to work every morning, including myself.

## "Those up there" live in their own world

A lot of people feel the politicians at the top have lost touch and the polls back that up. According to Ipsos, only 23 percent of Germans trust the federal government to act in the interest of people like them, while 47 percent don't. In a survey for the civil servants' union, only 17 percent still believe the state can do its job.

Someone I know once put it like this: as long as a politician works at the county level, they're close to people and "can do something". Once they move up to state or federal politics, it's a different world. Up there, party tactics, posts and their own careers seem to matter more than the worries of the people paying for all of it.

To be fair here too: this year the Bundestag skipped its automatic pay raise of almost €500 a month (the fact that this is even a thing made me jump from my chair while doing research). Several state parliaments raised their members' pay anyway, often automatically and without a vote.

## German punctuality is a myth, at least on the train

Abroad, Germans are known for being on time. Our trains are not. In September, only 52.4 percent of long-distance trains arrived on time and "on time" here means less than **six minutes** late. **Cancelled trains don't even show up in that number.** In 2015 the rate was still 74.4 percent. Deutsche Bahn's own target for 2026 was 60 percent and it's going to miss it.

## The fax machine lives on

About 70 percent of German government offices and companies still have a fax machine. When it comes to digital public services, Germany ranks 22nd out of 27 in the EU. Malta is first, followed by Estonia and Finland. If you've ever tried to register an address or apply for anything here, you know it often still means paper, an appointment and waiting.

## You pay for public TV whether you watch it or not

Every household in Germany pays a mandatory broadcasting fee for the public channels ARD and ZDF, whether anyone in the household watches them or not. Most people still call it "GEZ", after the agency that used to collect it. The fee is currently €18.36 a month. An independent commission recommends raising it to €18.64 from January 1, 2027. ARD and ZDF have taken the states to the Constitutional Court because the states blocked an earlier increase and the ruling is still pending.

I don't even watch TV and I'm luckily still excluded because of my living accomodation.

## A birthday letter from the army

Since January 2026, every 18-year-old gets a letter from the Bundeswehr (the German armed forces) with a QR code that leads to an online questionnaire about their willingness and fitness to serve. For young men it's mandatory, for women it's optional (Gender equality my ass).

Starting July 1, 2027, compulsory medical exams will be phased in for men born in 2008 or later (I'm luckily not in that group, but I have one family member, who is not excluded here...). Anyone who ignores the summons can be brought in by the police or assessed based on their file. Officially it's not a return of conscription, but it's a lot closer to one than most people abroad realize.

That's a glimpse of what being young in Germany looks like right now. There's a lot more I could write about, like housing costs or the crumbling bridges or even a lot more about German politicy, but this is already long enough.
